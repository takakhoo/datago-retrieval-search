\title{Mikiri: Knowing When a Frozen Go Engine Has Searched Enough}

\author{
    Anonymous submission

\begin{abstract}
A Go engine in the AlphaZero family gives every move the same search budget. We study how much stronger a frozen engine becomes when only that allocation changes. Mikiri wraps KataGo, leaves its network and search untouched, and climbs a short ladder of visit budgets, stopping at the first rung where the winrate still at stake no longer pays for the next rung. We build a dataset of 6{,}000 positions searched at seven budgets, with every candidate move scored by an independent forced search, and use it to train the stopping rule, to forecast match strength, and to compare against ten published stopping rules re-implemented on the same engine. Offline, Mikiri matches uniform search at 1.8 to 2.1 times the visits, ahead of the best prior rule by \pairedVmctsTwo{} in a paired test. In play against KataGo at 200 visits per move with the same network, it gains $\longElo$ Elo (95\% interval $\longEloLo$ to $\longEloHi$) at equal visits over \longGames{} games, where doubling KataGo's own budget gains $\unidoubleElo$. Dividing the predicted stake by the price of the next rung is the decisive ingredient: it turns stopping signals that are worse than no gate into gains, and it improves prior learned rules as well. We also report two negative results: approximate retrieval of stored searches does not transfer between similar positions, and an exact search memory saves visits but almost no network evaluations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Engines in the AlphaGo line choose a move by a tree search guided by a policy and value network \cite{silver2016mastering,silver2017mastering}, and they are compared at a fixed number of search visits per move. KataGo \cite{wu2019accelerating}, the strongest openly available engine of this kind, follows the convention: at 200 visits per move, a forced recapture and a whole-board fight receive the same 200 visits.

How much does that uniformity cost? Classical work on time management for Monte-Carlo tree search reports large gains from spending more time on some moves than on others \cite{huang2010time,baier2016time}. For neural engines the evidence comes from small boards and small networks \cite{lan2021learning,ye2022spending,muppidi2026finding}, and KataGo already adapts its search inside the tree \cite{katago_methods}. Whether a per-move budget decision still helps a modern, strong engine on the full board is an open empirical question.

We answer it with a wrapper, Mikiri, that changes nothing but the number of visits a move receives. It searches at 50 visits and asks a small model whether the decision is settled. If not, it searches at the next rung of a ladder, and asks again. The model predicts the winrate the current choice may still be giving up, and search stops when that stake, divided by the visits the next rung would add, falls below a threshold.

Our contributions are these.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A testbed with exact accounting.} Both players use the same engine, network and move-selection rule. Compute is reported as visits, as visits with every restarted search charged, and as network evaluations measured per player.
\item \textbf{A ladder dataset with independent labels.} 6{,}000 positions, seven budgets each, every chosen move scored by a separate forced search. It shows that search error is rare and concentrated, and it forecasts match Elo.
\item \textbf{A stopping rule that is worth nearly a doubling of search.} $\longElo$ Elo over KataGo at equal visits, and $\strictElo$ Elo with 20\% fewer.
\item \textbf{A comparison with ten published rules} on one engine, offline and, for the strongest two, in play.
\item \textbf{The rate rule.} Pricing the next rung matters more than the model, and improves prior signals.
\item \textbf{Two negative results} on retrieval for a frozen engine.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Deciding how long to search.} Huang \textit{et al.}~\shortcite{huang2010time} extend search when the most-visited move is not the highest-valued one. Baier and Winands~\shortcite{baier2016time} find that stopping early when the leader cannot be overtaken is the most reliable of several strategies, and Leela Chess Zero ships a variant of it together with a stop on the KL gain of the visit distribution \cite{lc0_options}. Hay \textit{et al.}~\shortcite{hay2012selecting} and Kaufmann and Koolen~\shortcite{kaufmann2017monte} give stopping rules with a value-of-information and a best-arm-identification rationale. For AlphaZero-style agents, DS-MCTS \cite{lan2021learning} trains networks to predict whether the current choice will be worse than the final one, V-MCTS \cite{ye2022spending} stops when virtually expanded policies have converged, and Muppidi \textit{et al.}~\shortcite{muppidi2026finding} learn a budget chosen before search. These neural results are on 9$\times$9 boards or small planners. We test all of them on 19$\times$19 with a strong network (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\textbf{Reusing search.} Transposition tables and graph search share statistics inside one search \cite{childs2008transpositions,czech2021improving}; opening books do so across games \cite{katagobooks}. Retrieval of similar positions helps agents whose networks are trained to read the neighbours \cite{humphreys2022retrieval,goyal2022retrieval} and inside a single search \cite{xiao2018memory}. We ask what a frozen engine can reuse.

\section{Testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}

\textbf{Engine and rules.} Both players query KataGo v1.18.2 through its analysis engine. Each query is an independent search with an exact visit limit and one search thread, so no tree is inherited between moves and a visit means the same thing at every budget. The network is the 18-block \texttt{b18c384nbt} unless stated. Games use area scoring, positional superko and komi 7, the value at which the engine rates the empty board as even. Both players select moves as KataGo does by default, sampling in proportion to visits$^{1/T}$ with $T$ decaying from 0.5 to 0.1. A player resigns after three consecutive turns below 3\% winrate.

\textbf{Compute.} Mikiri searches a position at 50 visits and then perhaps at 200. With one thread and cached evaluations the shallow tree is a prefix of the deep one: on fresh engines, a 200-visit search followed by a 400-visit search evaluated 366 to 396 network rows, against 353 to 395 for the 400-visit search alone. We nevertheless report three measures: \emph{visits}, the size of the deepest search per move; \emph{restart visits}, the sum of all searches requested; and \emph{evaluations}, network rows executed by each player's own engine process. Budgets are matched on visits.

\textbf{Matches.} A match is a series of games from the empty board with alternating colours. Intervals are bootstrap percentiles over colour-balanced pairs. KataGo against itself at 200 visits scores \unisameScore\% over \unisameGames{} games ($\unisameElo$ Elo).

\section{The Ladder Dataset}

We played 300 self-play games at budgets drawn log-uniformly from 30 to 200 visits and sampled 20 positions per game. Each position is searched at 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1{,}600 and 3{,}200 visits. A search cannot grade itself, so for every distinct move any rung chose we run a separate 1{,}600-visit search restricted to that move at the root. Its winrate $q(s,a)$ is our value of playing $a$. The \emph{regret} of stopping at rung $k$ is $r_k(s)=\max_{a} q(s,a)-q(s,a_k(s))$, where $a_k$ is the move chosen at rung $k$ and the maximum is over scored moves. For a policy with mean regret $\bar r$ we report the uniform budget with the same mean regret, interpolating the uniform curve in log visits. The ratio of that budget to the policy's own mean cost is its \emph{compute multiplier}.

\textbf{Regret is rare and concentrated.} At 200 visits the engine gives up 0.99\% of winrate per move, and 71\% of positions carry no regret. The worst tenth hold 84\% of it (Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}). Regret averages 0.19\% per move over the first 40 moves and 1.4 to 1.6\% after move 100. A stopper that knew the true regret would reach a multiplier of 12 at a 200-visit budget.

\section{Mikiri}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering

\caption{How Mikiri decides one move.}
\label{fig:pipeline}

\textbf{The rate rule.} Let the ladder have rungs $v_1<\dots<v_m$, and let a stopping policy $\pi$ stop position $s$ at rung $\pi(s)$. We want the policy that minimises expected regret subject to an expected cost,
\begin{equation}
  \min_\pi \; \mathbb{E}\bigl[r_{\pi(s)}(s)\bigr] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbb{E}\bigl[v_{\pi(s)}\bigr] \le B .
\end{equation}
With a multiplier $\lambda$ on the constraint, the problem separates over positions: each position minimises $r_k(s)+\lambda v_k$. At rung $j$ the visits already spent cannot be recovered, and continuing by one rung is worthwhile exactly when $\mathbb{E}[r_j-r_{j+1}\mid x] > \lambda\,(v_{j+1}-v_j)$, where $x$ is what the finished search shows. The reduction $r_j-r_{j+1}$ is at most $r_j$. Replacing it by a prediction $\hat r_j(x)$ of the regret remaining gives the one-step rule: stop when
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\hat r_j(x)}{v_{j+1}-v_j} < \lambda .
  \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
One $\lambda$ prices every decision on the ladder alike. On a geometric ladder the next rung costs more each time, so going from 800 to 3{,}200 visits needs four times the stake that going from 200 to 800 does. A flat threshold on $\hat r_j$ ignores that price, and spends most of a long ladder's budget on a few positions that no amount of search resolves.

\textbf{The model.} $\hat r$ is a gradient-boosted tree ensemble (150 trees of depth 3) fitted to the square root of regret, with all non-final rungs pooled. Its 30 inputs come from the finished search at no engine cost: how contested the root is (visit shares, entropy, the margin between the best move's lower confidence bound and its best rival), how the search disagrees with the network (priors, KL from prior to visits, raw against searched winrate), what is at stake (winrate, distance from 50\%, score), KataGo's own raw error predictions, game phase, and what changed since the previous rung. All offline numbers are cross-fitted by game.

\textbf{The ledger.} Mikiri is granted the baseline's budget $B$ for every move, and a ledger records what it spends. The offline frontier gives a map $\lambda(c)$ from a target mean cost $c$ to a threshold. After each move the controller sets the next target to $B$ plus the ledger's balance spread over a horizon of 3{,}000 moves, and divides it by a running ratio of realised to commanded spending, which corrects for the difference between the dataset and real games. Spending tracks the grant to within 1\% in every match we report.

\textbf{Memory.} Finished searches from the first 80 moves are stored under a key that is invariant to the eight board symmetries and to move order. A later hit is played from the stored search at zero visits.

\section{Offline Results}

\begin{figure}[t]

\textbf{Multiplier.} On the four-rung ladder (50, 200, 800, 3{,}200) the stopper at a mean cost of 200 visits loses as little as uniform search at 358, a multiplier of 1.80, and stays between 1.69 and 1.88 from 100 to 800 visits (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). With every restart charged it is 1.29 to 1.51. On the full doubling ladder it reaches 2.13.

\textbf{What matters.} Removing the trajectory features, KataGo's raw error predictions, or all but seven features changes the multiplier at 200 visits by at most 0.09, and 300 labeled positions already give 1.50. Fitting the square root of regret instead of regret raises it from 1.74 to 1.82. Replacing the rate rule by a flat threshold lowers it to 1.41. Figure~\ref{fig:rate} shows the same contrast for hand-written signals: with a flat threshold the entropy gate of an earlier prototype is worse than no gate (0.63), and with the rate rule it is a gain (1.16).

\subsection{Comparison with Prior Rules}
\label{sec:baselines}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering\small

\caption{Compute multipliers on the doubling ladder (above 1 is better than uniform search), with 95\% bootstrap intervals over games. \label{tab:baselines}

We re-implemented ten published rules from their papers and source code (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). All see the same root statistics and are scored by the same forced values. \emph{BEHIND} and \emph{UNST} search longer when the player is losing or when the most-visited move is not the highest-valued one \cite{huang2010time}. \emph{STOP} ends a search once the runner-up cannot overtake the leader in the visits that remain, or in a fraction $p$ of them, and \emph{CLOSE} extends it when the top two moves are close in visits \cite{baier2016time}. \emph{Smart pruning} is Leela Chess Zero's form of STOP, shipped with a stop on the per-visit KL gain of the visit distribution \cite{lc0_options}. The \emph{VOI} rule stops when a bound on the value of further samples falls below their cost \cite{hay2012selecting}, and the \emph{BAI} rule when the leader's confidence interval separates from every rival's \cite{kaufmann2017monte}. The \emph{state-only} rule predicts regret from features available before any search, in the spirit of Muppidi \textit{et al.}~\shortcite{muppidi2026finding}. Two learned rules are adaptations: for DS-MCTS we train a classifier on root statistics to the authors' label $U(s,n)$, in place of their board-input networks, and for V-MCTS we run the authors' virtual expansion on KataGo's root priors, visits and winrates with their published $r=0.2$ and $\epsilon=0.1$. Every rule is charged continuation cost, which is what a rule inside the engine pays. Charged for every restart, Mikiri's four-rung ladder reaches 1.44 at 200 visits, below the 1.60 of V-MCTS, which has no restarts to charge; the matches settle that question in network evaluations.

Three findings. First, the rules with a statistical stopping guarantee are far worse than uniform search here, because they stop on confidence in the leading move and ignore whether the game is still in doubt. Second, V-MCTS is the strongest prior rule (1.60 at 200 visits), and Mikiri exceeds it by \pairedVmctsTwo{} at 200 visits and \pairedVmctsFour{} at 400, paired over the same resampled games. Third, the rate rule improves every signal it can be applied to, including the DS-MCTS classifier (1.48 to 1.75), which with it ties Mikiri at 400 visits (\pairedDsRateFour).

\textbf{What to predict, and how to price it.} Remaining regret is an upper bound on what continuing can recover. Table~\ref{tab:targets} compares it with the quantities the derivation names: the gain from the next rung, the gain from the whole ladder, and the best recovery per visit over any continuation, $\max_{k>j}(r_j-r_k)/(v_k-v_j)$. Read by an oracle, that last index reproduces the hindsight optimum (14.11 against 14.16). Learned from search statistics it trails remaining regret under the rate rule (1.63 to 1.87 against 1.83 to 2.14). The next-rung gain is weakest: myopic as an oracle, noisy as a target. Pricing remaining regret by the visits to the top rung is worse than no price. The rate rule pairs a target that search statistics can predict with the one cost that is known exactly.

\textbf{Do prior rules see more?} We fed the KLD gain, the VOI bound, the BAI gap and the V-MCTS distance to Mikiri's regressor as extra inputs, singly and together. The multiplier at 200 visits moved from 2.14 to between 2.07 and 2.17, with no paired gain distinguishable from zero. The rules differ in how they use the root statistics; the statistics themselves are exhausted.

\section{What a Frozen Engine Can Retrieve}

\textbf{Exact memory.} Positions recur: across 300 self-play games, a position at move 20 had occurred in an earlier game, up to symmetry, half of the time. In play, memory alone served \memonlyHitRate\% of moves over \memonlyGames{} games with strength unchanged ($\memonlyElo$ Elo, $\memonlyEloLo$ to $\memonlyEloHi$) at \memonlyVisits{} visits per move. It did not reduce network evaluations (\memonlyEvals{} per move against \memonlyBaseEvals): KataGo caches evaluations for the life of its process, so the positions memory serves were already cheap.

\textbf{Approximate retrieval.} We embed positions with the raw policy and ownership maps, align over the eight symmetries, and offer the nearest stored position's 3{,}200-visit move as a proposal. Where the 200-visit and 3{,}200-visit moves differ (1{,}605 positions), the nearest neighbour's move is right 5.5\% of the time, against 43\% for the policy network's own best alternative. A neighbour's move matches in 96\% of cases at similarity above 0.999, which is the same position, and in 27\% at 0.90 to 0.97. Neighbours' regret predicts a position's regret with AUC 0.55.

\section{Limitations}

Results are for KataGo networks at 100 to 800 visits, against KataGo, where a doubling of visits is worth about 230 Elo; larger budgets are untested. Regret is defined by a search and inherits its blind spots, though the matches do not depend on it. Of the regret that survives a 200-visit search, 57\% sits on moves that search gave under 10\% of its visits, which bounds what any rule reading root statistics can recover. Our versions of prior rules are re-implementations on a different engine than their authors used. A stopping rule built on the engine's own statistics offers no protection against adversarial policies \cite{wang2023adversarial}.

\section{Conclusion}

A frozen Go engine leaves most of a doubling of search on the table by spending visits uniformly. A small learned stopping rule recovers it, and the part of the rule that matters most is the price it puts on continuing.
