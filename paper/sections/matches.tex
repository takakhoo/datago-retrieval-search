\subsection{What a visit is worth}

To read the other results we first need the exchange rate between compute and strength in this setting. KataGo against itself at 200 visits scores \unisameScore\% over \unisameGames{} games ($\unisameElo$ Elo, $\unisameEloLo$ to $\unisameEloHi$), which checks that the harness has no side bias. Against the 200-visit engine, KataGo at 100 visits rates $\unihalfElo$ Elo, at 283 visits $\unirootElo$, and at 400 visits $\unidoubleElo$. A doubling is worth about 230 Elo here. In network evaluations the uniform engine runs \unihalfEvals, \unisameEvals, \unirootEvals, and \unidoubleEvals{} per move at the four budgets: fewer than its visits, because consecutive searches share cached evaluations.

\subsection{The stopping rule at equal visits and below}

With the ladder 50, 200, 800, 3{,}200 and the controller holding its mean at the opponent's budget, Mikiri scores \mainScore\% over \mainGames{} games (\mainRecord), a gain of $\mainElo$ Elo (95\% interval $\mainEloLo$ to $\mainEloHi$), at \mainVisits{} visits per move. Counting every restarted search it requested \mainRestart{} visits per move, and it ran \mainEvals{} network evaluations per move to KataGo's \mainBaseEvals.

A longer ladder does at least as well. With six rungs (50, 200, 400, 800, 1{,}600, 3{,}200), the best ladder offline by continuation cost, Mikiri scores \longScore\% over \longGames{} games (\longRecord), a gain of $\longElo$ Elo ($\longEloLo$ to $\longEloHi$), at \longVisits{} visits per move. KataGo gains $\unidoubleElo$ from doubling its budget, so the rule recovers nearly all of a doubling.\footnote{After 600 games this match stood at $+263$. The first 600 games ran above the long-run rate, and the figure here is for all 1{,}000.} The finer ladder restarts more often, so it requested \longRestart{} visits per move with restarts counted, and it ran \longEvals{} evaluations per move to KataGo's \longBaseEvals. The two ladders' intervals overlap, so the match does not separate them, though the ladder dataset ranks the six-rung ladder higher (Table~\ref{tab:prediction}). Unless we say otherwise, the analyses below use the four-rung match, which has the most games and a full trace.

Visits understate Mikiri's cost in one respect. A fixed-budget engine re-meets much of its previous tree on the next move and finds those evaluations cached. Mikiri's searches vary in size, so less carries over, and it runs more network evaluations per visit. We therefore reran the match with Mikiri granted fewer visits. At a grant of 160 it averaged \strictVisits{} visits per move, \strictRestart{} with every restarted search counted, and \strictEvals{} network evaluations to KataGo's \strictBaseEvals. It scored \strictScore\% over \strictGames{} games (\strictRecord), a gain of $\strictElo$ Elo ($\strictEloLo$ to $\strictEloHi$). At a grant of 140 it averaged \leanVisits{} visits, \leanRestart{} restart visits, and \leanEvals{} evaluations to KataGo's \leanBaseEvals, and gained $\leanElo$ Elo ($\leanEloLo$ to $\leanEloHi$) over \leanGames{} games.

Figure~\ref{fig:elo} places these results against the uniform curve under all three measures of compute. Every Mikiri run lies above the curve under each. The figure also shows the prior rules of Section~\ref{sec:priorplay}. The comparison at equal evaluations is direct: Mikiri in the main match and KataGo at 283 visits run \mainEvals{} and \unirootEvals{} evaluations per move and gain $\mainElo$ and $\unirootElo$ Elo.

\subsection{The offline analysis predicted the result}

The ladder dataset gives a forecast that can be checked. For each run we take the mean visits Mikiri actually used, read the cross-fitted regret at that cost from Table~\ref{tab:frontier}, convert it to equivalent uniform visits, and read the Elo of that uniform budget from the measured curve. Table~\ref{tab:prediction} compares the forecast with the match.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small

\caption{Elo forecast from the offline regret analysis, against the Elo measured in play, for stopping rules against KataGo at 200 visits.}
\label{tab:prediction}

For the four-rung match at 200 visits the forecast is within nine Elo of the result, and for the six-rung ladder within three. At 160 visits it is about 30 Elo too high, outside the measured interval. For the two prior rules of Section~\ref{sec:priorplay}, forecast from their own calibration on the ladder dataset, it is ten Elo too high and 18 too low, both inside the intervals. The forecast orders Mikiri's three configurations correctly and puts both prior rules below Mikiri's equal-visit matches, as measured. It has the two prior rules 17 Elo apart in one order and the matches have them 11 apart in the other, a difference neither can resolve. Regret measured on self-play positions is a usable guide to match strength, good to a few tens of Elo, which makes the ladder dataset a cheap way to choose among stopping rules before playing any games.

\subsection{Prior rules in play}
\label{sec:priorplay}

Section~\ref{sec:baselines} compares ten published stopping rules offline. We packaged the two strongest so that they run in the same player, under the same controller, at the same 200-visit budget. The V-MCTS rule \citep{ye2022spending}, with the authors' constants and their ladder of checkpoints up to 400 visits, gains $\vmctsElo$ Elo ($\vmctsEloLo$ to $\vmctsEloHi$) over \vmctsGames{} games at \vmctsVisits{} visits per move. The DS-MCTS-style classifier \citep{lan2021learning} gains $\dsmctsElo$ Elo ($\dsmctsEloLo$ to $\dsmctsEloHi$) over \dsmctsGames{} games.

The DS-MCTS-style rule uses the full doubling ladder and ran \dsmctsEvals{} evaluations per move to KataGo's \dsmctsBaseEvals, slightly more than Mikiri's six-rung ladder (\longEvals), for about 80 Elo less. Mikiri is ahead of it on every measure of compute.

At equal visits Mikiri is also ahead of V-MCTS: $\mainElo$ and $\longElo$ against $\vmctsElo$, with intervals that do not overlap. Network evaluations tell a closer story. V-MCTS ran \vmctsEvals{} evaluations per move to KataGo's \vmctsBaseEvals, and Mikiri's four-rung ladder ran \mainEvals. Mikiri's ladder reaches 3{,}200 visits, and its deepest searches meet fewer cached positions than searches capped at 400. Interpolating between Mikiri's matches at 160 and 200 visits (\strictEvals{} and \mainEvals{} evaluations, $\strictElo$ and $\mainElo$ Elo) puts it at about $+135$ at \vmctsEvals{} evaluations, level with V-MCTS. On this evidence Mikiri is the better rule per visit and the two are indistinguishable per network evaluation. Two further matches test this directly. In the first, Mikiri's stopper runs on V-MCTS's own ladder (50, 100, 200, 400), where it reaches 1.80 offline to V-MCTS's 1.60, so that the two rules differ in nothing else: it gains $\shortElo$ Elo ($\shortEloLo$ to $\shortEloHi$) at \shortEvals{} evaluations per move. In the second, the four-rung ladder is granted 175 visits to bring its evaluations close to those of V-MCTS: it gains $\midElo$ Elo ($\midEloLo$ to $\midEloHi$) at \midEvals{} evaluations per move.

\subsection{In-game ablations}

Two hand-written rules were played on the same ladder at the same budget. The LCB margin with the rate rule, which reaches 1.45 offline with no learning, gains $\rulelcbElo$ Elo ($\rulelcbEloLo$ to $\rulelcbEloHi$) over \rulelcbGames{} games. The entropy gate of the first version with a flat threshold, 0.63 offline, gains $\vonegateElo$ Elo ($\vonegateEloLo$ to $\vonegateEloHi$) over \vonegateGames{} games.

\subsection{Without move sampling}

KataGo's default play samples among well-visited moves early in the game. A search that stops when one move dominates has sharper visit counts than a fixed-budget search, so sampling could favour Mikiri for reasons unrelated to search quality. To rule this out we sampled 400 balanced openings of 12 moves, played each twice with colours swapped, and had both players always play their engine's top move. Mikiri scores \pairedScore\% over \pairedGames{} games (\pairedRecord), $\pairedElo$ Elo ($\pairedEloLo$ to $\pairedEloHi$), at \pairedVisits{} visits per move.

\subsection{Where the visits go}

Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows Mikiri's spending in the main match. It uses about a third of the budget over the first 20 moves, where Section~\ref{sec:offline} found almost no regret to recover, and about 270 visits per move around move 140. After move 60 it also spends by stakes: about 130 visits per move when it rates its own winrate above 90\%, about 160 when below 10\%, and 190 to 330 in between, with the peak when it is behind but not lost. Neither behaviour was programmed. Both follow from predicting regret in winrate units. Of all its moves, 47\% stopped at 50 visits, 42\% at 200, 11\% at 800, and 0.2\% at 3{,}200.

\subsection{Memory}

With memory alone and no stopper, Mikiri is KataGo with an opening book that fills itself. Over \memonlyGames{} games it served \memonlyHitRate\% of its moves from memory (\memonlyHitLate\% in the second half of the series, from \memonlyMemory{} stored positions), averaged \memonlyVisits{} visits per move, and scored \memonlyScore\% ($\memonlyElo$ Elo, $\memonlyEloLo$ to $\memonlyEloHi$). Strength is unchanged, as it should be when a hit returns the search that would have been run, and a fifth of the visits are saved.

The saving does not carry over to network evaluations. Mikiri ran \memonlyEvals{} evaluations per move and KataGo \memonlyBaseEvals. KataGo keeps a cache of network evaluations for as long as its process lives, so a baseline that plays hundreds of games in one process has already paid for the positions that recur: its opening searches cost visits but few new evaluations. Exact memory removes search work that the evaluation cache had already made cheap. It would save evaluations for an engine that restarts between games, and it saves the tree-search overhead in any case, but in this harness its effect on the dominant cost is nil.

The full system combines memory with the stopper and lets the controller reinvest what memory saves. Over \fullGames{} games it scores \fullScore\% (\fullRecord), $\fullElo$ Elo ($\fullEloLo$ to $\fullEloHi$), at \fullVisits{} visits and \fullEvals{} evaluations per move against \fullBaseEvals, with \fullHitRate\% of moves served from a memory that grew to \fullMemory{} positions. The stopper alone gained $\mainElo$ ($\mainEloLo$ to $\mainEloHi$) at \mainEvals{} evaluations per move. The intervals overlap, and the reinvested visits are new searches that the evaluation cache does not cover, which is why evaluations rise. Memory adds little on top of the stopper in this setting.

\subsection{Other budgets, another network, other boards}

The same model, trained once on 19$\times$19 positions from the b18 network, was run without retraining in further settings.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Budgets.} Against KataGo at 100, 400, and 800 visits, at matched visits, Mikiri gains $\bhundredElo$ ($\bhundredEloLo$ to $\bhundredEloHi$), $\bfourElo$ ($\bfourEloLo$ to $\bfourEloHi$), and $\beightElo$ ($\beightEloLo$ to $\beightEloHi$) Elo. At 100 visits the first rung of the ladder is already half the budget, which leaves the rule less to reallocate than at 200.
\item \textbf{A stronger network.} On the 28-block network, with both players using it, Mikiri gains $\bignetElo$ ($\bignetEloLo$ to $\bignetEloHi$) Elo.
\item \textbf{Smaller boards.} On 13$\times$13 and 9$\times$9 it gains $\thirteenElo$ ($\thirteenEloLo$ to $\thirteenEloHi$) and $\nineElo$ ($\nineEloLo$ to $\nineEloHi$) Elo.
\end{itemize}
